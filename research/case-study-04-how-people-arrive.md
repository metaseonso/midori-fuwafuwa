# Case Study 04 — How People Arrive

## The question

Everything built so far points inward. The Fields are designed, the Forest architecture is
settled, the design system is in progress, the tooling is ranked, the protection order of
operations is written. The audience question was answered in the sense that it was *named* —
"building an audience for our original IP" — and then nothing was written about how a single
stranger ever ends up on the site.

This case study asks the outward question. Not "how do we market," which is the wrong question
for two artists who said plainly that they want *"whatever is less on us so we can remain
creative and express ourselves in a way that gets appreciated over time."* The right question is
narrower and more answerable:

**What does the studio put into the world that a stranger can meet, and does that thing carry the
address home?**

---

## The recommendation, first

### The headline finding

Across a dozen verified cases of illustrated original IP reaching a real audience, only **three
distinct growth mechanisms** appear. "Built an audience by posting consistently" is the weakest
of them, and the two that produced the biggest outcomes did not involve an audience at all.

| Archetype | Cases | Open in 2026? |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Messaging-keyboard distribution** (stickers and GIFs injected into private conversations) | Pusheen, Chibird, Bugcat Capoo | **Closed** — slot scarcity is gone |
| **B — Feed / organic-reach arbitrage** (a dated, time-limited platform window) | Catana Comics, Sarah's Scribbles, Chiikawa, Puuung | **Closed** — every window has shut, and each closing can be dated |
| **C — B2B rights market and owned distribution** (no audience input whatsoever) | Hilda, Labubu, Dav Pilkey, Jon Klassen, Oliver Jeffers | **Fully open** |

The uncomfortable conclusion, and the one that should govern the plan: **the mechanisms still
open in 2026 are the ones that never depended on an audience.**

A second finding, equally load-bearing: **not one creator in this study monetised on the platform
that gave them their audience.** Catana Chetwynd states it flatly — *"Instagram doesn't pay me
anything."* Every durable income in the study came from the same place: books, licensing, rights,
subscriptions, and physical goods.

### The two paths, and which one to build for

**Path 1 — the intermediary path.** A distributor with its own machinery picks the character up
and carries it to people who have never heard of the artist. Facebook's sticker keyboard
(Pusheen). LINE's near-empty 2014 store (Capoo). Kodansha (Chiikawa). Pop Mart's blind-box retail
(Labubu). Andrews McMeel (Sarah's Scribbles). Silvergate and Netflix (Hilda). Scholastic Book
Fairs (Dav Pilkey). Enormous ceiling, lottery odds, and — critically — **it cannot be worked at
directly, only made ready for.**

**Path 2 — the direct path.** Smaller ceiling, far better odds, fully compatible with the stance.
Three verified proofs, and they are the most encouraging material in the research:

- **Chibird (Jacqueline Chen)** supports a full-time career on **574,000 followers** — an order of
  magnitude below Catana, two below Pusheen — because the revenue is a $5 Patreon led by physical
  goods, a Shopify store, and book advances. **None of it is reach-dependent.** She went full-time
  in **late 2021 — after every growth window in this study had already closed.** Hers is the only
  business model here that could be started from scratch today.
- **Puuung (Park Dami)** is the single most important 2026 datapoint in the study. Her 2015
  Kickstarter raised **$126,644 from 1,828 backers**. Her February–March **2026** campaign, *Witch
  BIBI*, raised **$100,238 from 1,717 backers**. Eleven years apart, essentially the same people —
  and in between, the platform that launched her (Naver's Grafolio) dismantled its entire creator
  economy. **The direct channel survived total platform collapse.**
- **Craig Mod**, a photographer and book-maker at about this scale: ~50,000 subscribers,
  **$730,000+** of books sold through his own shop since August 2020, over **$1,000,000** including
  memberships. And the detail that makes him the right model rather than merely an impressive one:
  **he checks his subscriber numbers once a year, at tax time,** and refuses to look at churn. He
  is proof that *owning* an audience and *surveilling* one are different things — which is exactly
  the distinction this studio's stance turns on.

**Recommendation: build Path 2, and make yourself legible to Path 1.** The Path-1 preparation is
not a marketing activity; it is having a coherent body of character work in places where a
publisher, an art-toy company or a rights agent can find it. Which is the same work.

### Do these

**1. A no-tracking seasonal letter, and a shop that exists before it is needed.**
Four sends a year, hosted off-site on the provider's own domain so the studio site keeps its
zero-form footing, all open- and click-tracking off. This is the only channel the studio controls
and the only one that survives an account ban or a platform shutdown.

The "shop first" half comes from the most transferable detail in the Pusheen story. **Claire Belton
was manufacturing and selling plush direct-to-consumer in 2011 — two years before Facebook's
sticker store arrived.** When luck came, there was something to convert it into. The lesson is not
the lucky slot; it is the sequencing.

And the principle costs almost nothing, because open tracking is already fictional: Litmus's July
2026 data puts Apple at **62.26%** of email client market share, with Mail Privacy Protection
affecting roughly **55–60% of all opens.** Turning off tracking in 2026 declines noise, not data.

**2. Reddit — the one cold-start ignition venue still open, used deliberately and rarely.**
This is a correction to the conventional advice and it is well evidenced. **Catana Comics did not
start on Instagram.** Her partner posted her comics to **r/comics in November 2016 without asking**;
it went viral with 2,000+ comments, and she went from zero to roughly **250,000 followers in six
months.** Reddit is the only venue in this study where a zero-follower account can win on the
artifact alone, with no graph, no history and no cadence.

Use it the way the evidence supports: **one exceptional self-contained image, posted deliberately,
maybe twice a year.** Not a presence. Not a habit. The tolerance for repeat self-promotion is low
and the cost of being read as a marketer is disproportionate.

**3. Instagram — capture, not discovery.**
Catana's actual model was two-stage: **Reddit for discovery, Instagram for capture.** Her stated
reason for giving press her social links rather than her website was that it converts to
"long-term followers instead of just one-time viewers." That function still works. Growth does
not — see A.3 for the numbers, which are stark.

Instagram is also where the indie illustrated-IP cohort migrated after the comics platforms locked
solo creators out of the monetisable genres, and where publishers and licensors check that a studio
is real. One post a month, the month's finished artifact. No Reels. Do not measure it.

**Free, because it costs nothing:** ship RSS on day one and pipe it into a Bluesky account
automatically. Zero marginal cost once the feed exists, and it is the plumbing for the letter.

### Two things demoted on the evidence

**Stickers are no longer a discovery channel.** This is worth stating because the Pusheen and Capoo
cases invite the opposite reading, and the numbers are decisive. Yara shipped Capoo's 40-sticker
set in **October 2014, month five** of LINE's individual creator market, into a catalogue of about
**100,000 sets from 390,000 creators** — and on grandfathered terms paying **50% of gross**. By
June 2025 the market held **over 1,000,000 creators and roughly 10,000,000 sticker sets**, and
anyone registering after 1 February 2015 receives 50% of *net* after a 30% platform fee. **Roughly
a hundredfold catalogue inflation against fixed storefront attention, plus a ~30% economic
haircut.** Pusheen's slot was even less repeatable: Facebook *approached Belton in January 2013*,
and at the April 2013 launch Pusheen was one of only **seven** free sticker sets available.

A set is still worth making eventually — as a distribution surface and a cheap market test — but
it belongs in Season 3 as an experiment, not in the audience plan.

**Pinterest is a reasonable structural bet, not an evidenced one.** It is the only major platform
that behaves like a search engine rather than a feed — a pin keeps surfacing for years, it sends
outbound clicks by design, and its unit is a single finished illustration, so a seasonal batch
upload is a correct use of it rather than a compromise. That argument holds on structure. But no
case in this research grew through Pinterest, and its current scale figures could not be verified.
Treat it as a low-cost bet worth four batch uploads a year, not as a pillar.

### Do not do these

- **No daily posting anywhere.** Any channel whose reach decays during dormancy is structurally
  incompatible with a clock that has a rest quarter in it. That rules out TikTok, YouTube, and
  Instagram-as-growth by construction, not preference.
- **No Webtoon or Tapas.** The clearest negative in the research, and the numbers are brutal —
  see A.4 and B.4.
- **No Substack.** It cannot be made consistent with the stance — see C.3.
- **No email-capture funnel, no lead magnet, no retargeting, no paid ads.**
- **No cross-posting to five platforms.** Every case in this study concentrated.

### And say the slow part plainly

Kasing Lung had been a working picture-book illustrator for roughly **fifteen years** — Culturecom
Hong Kong from 1995, then children's books for the Belgian publisher De Eenhoorn, winning its
illustration award in 2003 — before any of this. He moved into art toys with How2Work from 2010,
and **that line underperformed commercially for three years.** Labubu and *The Monsters* debuted in
2014–15. Pop Mart licensed it in **2019**. It became a global phenomenon in **April 2024**, when
Blackpink's Lisa was photographed with a keychain.

Note what did the work, and what did not. Not the picture books. Not social media, which played no
role at any point. **Physical retail scarcity and one celebrity photograph.** A studio launching a
children's book series should hold that without flinching: the book is the seed and the proof, and
it is unlikely to be the thing that finds the audience.

There is no growth hack here because the research does not contain one. The studio's real advantage
is not a channel — it is that it has already committed to a rhythm designed to be sustained for the
length of time this actually takes.

## Part A — What actually works for illustrated original IP

### A.1 The three archetypes, with the cases

**Archetype A — the messaging keyboard.** Two of the three big cute-character successes here won
through the *message-compose UI*, not the feed. Private-message distribution does not decay against
feed ranking and has no impression ceiling.

- **Pusheen** (Claire Belton + Andrew Duff — **two people**). Everyday Cute launched May 2010 with
  Pusheen in the first strip. Tumblr built a seed of ~10,000 followers. **Facebook approached
  Belton in January 2013**; at the mid-April iOS launch of the sticker store, Pusheen was **one of
  seven free sets** — roughly one-in-seven of available inventory inside a billion-user compose
  window. Web rollout 2 July 2013. Facebook fans went from ~1M to over 9M in three years.
- **Chibird** (Jacqueline Chen, solo, started **2010 in her second year of high school**). Her
  publisher credits *"hundreds of millions of views on GIPHY."* A Chibird GIF surfaced for "hug" or
  "you got this" gets injected into private conversations across every app that embeds the GIPHY
  picker — iMessage, Slack, Discord, WhatsApp. Same archetype as Pusheen; nobody describes it that
  way in interviews.
- **Bugcat Capoo** (亞拉 / Yara, solo, Taiwan). Originated as an amateur strip on the **Bahamut
  forum, 16 May 2014**. LINE opened its Creators Market to individuals on 8 May 2014; Yara shipped
  40 stickers in **October 2014**. He later won the 2015 WEBTOON Original Comic Competition — a
  *second* platform-granted slot — and serialised through March 2020. Today: recurring 7-ELEVEN
  Taiwan campaigns (**90 SKUs**, May–June 2026), Capoo House in Taichung, Kaohsiung Metro branding.

**Archetype B — feed arbitrage.** A dated window, in every case now shut.

- **Catana Comics.** The correction matters: it **started on Reddit**, not Instagram or Tumblr. Her
  partner John Freed posted her comics to **r/comics in November 2016 without her permission**; it
  went viral. Reddit supplied discovery, Instagram supplied capture. Then Andrews McMeel: *Little
  Moments of Love* (June 2018, #3 nonfiction), *Snug* (Feb 2020) — which **sold 130,000 copies in
  two months and hit #1 New York Times bestseller** — and three more through January 2026.
- **Sarah's Scribbles.** Tumblr, 2013, while a student at MICA. The structurally important detail
  most accounts miss: the comic was originally called *Doodle Time*, and **GoComics required the
  rename as a condition of syndication.** GoComics is owned by Andrews McMeel — so she was inside
  the AMP pipeline *before* the books. Five collections 2016–2024, Goodreads Choice three years
  running (2016–2018).
- **Chiikawa** (ナガノ / Nagano). Twitter serialisation from **January 2020**; Kodansha tankōbon from
  February 2021 (**4.6 million copies cumulative by November 2025**); Doga Kobo anime premiered **4
  April 2022 on Fuji TV**; Toho film July 2026. But: **Nagano was not an unknown.** She had already
  built 自分ツッコミくま (award-winning, four books 2018–2020) and had work serialised in a Kodansha
  magazine. **Chiikawa was a proven professional's fourth character, not a debut.**
- **Puuung.** Began the *Love Is* series in 2014, posting to **Grafolio** (Naver's creator platform)
  on a fixed Tuesday/Friday cadence; spread internationally via Facebook and Pinterest as
  text-light, universally legible emotional vignettes.

**Archetype C — the rights market, with no audience input at all.**

- **Hilda** is the cleanest counterexample in the study. Luke Pearson's *Hildafolk* (2010) was
  commissioned into Nobrow's **17×23 series — a fixed 24-page low-cost format explicitly used as a
  slot for emerging talent.** The decisive event contains zero audience component: **Kurt Mueller
  at Silvergate Media contacted Sam Arthur at Nobrow**, pitched an animated series, and produced an
  animation test that convinced the publisher. Netflix greenlit in 2016; three seasons and a film,
  2018–2023; a Daytime Emmy for Outstanding Children's Animated Series in 2021. Arthur's own words:
  the show *"has turned Hilda into a mainstream character."* **Audience was the output, not the
  input.**
- **Labubu.** See "say the slow part plainly," above. Pop Mart's model is that **original artist IP
  was roughly 76.5% of its 2023 revenue**, developed with independent artists worldwide — Kenny
  Wong, Xiong Miao, Ayan Deng, Kasing Lung. Sony acquired film rights in November 2025, with Paul
  King directing. **This channel is actively open and actively signing.**
- **Dav Pilkey.** Won the National Written & Illustrated By… Awards Contest in **1986, aged 19**.
  The mechanism is **Scholastic Book Fairs** — over 97,000 fairs a year reaching 30 million-plus
  children, a $553.1M segment in FY2023 — a captive physical channel owned by his publisher,
  touching social media at zero points. Unreproducible by an independent creator, and worth naming
  precisely so nobody mistakes it for a content strategy.
- **Jon Klassen** — Sheridan animation, DreamWorks, *Coraline* concept art; debut illustration won
  a Governor General's Award; 2013 Caldecott plus 2014 Kate Greenaway, the first book ever to win
  both. **Oliver Jeffers** — no agent, *How to Catch a Star* was **whisked off HarperCollins'
  slush pile in 2004**, two years before Twitter existed. His large following is a *consequence* of
  the career, not a cause.

**Simon Stålenhag** sits across B and C and is the most transferable pattern of all. He posted
paintings with short captions on no schedule; **Boing Boing featured him on 21 August 2013** and
art-blog aggregators redistributed single images. Then: *Ur Varselklotet* (Fria Ligan, 2014, two
print runs sold out) → a **2015** Kickstarter for the English edition raising **$321,680 from 3,890
backers** → an RPG campaign (SEK 3.75m) → *The Electric State* (SEK 3.11m, 2016) → agent **Julia
Angelin at Salomonsson Agency** selling into **15+ languages** → Amazon Prime series (April 2020)
→ Netflix film (March 2025). **A rights agent, not a follower count, produced the screen deals.**

### A.2 What the pattern says

**The unit of discovery is a single image, not an account.** Pusheen spread as individual stickers,
Sarah's Scribbles as reblogged single strips, Stålenhag as individual paintings, Catana as one
Reddit post. Each artifact needed no context, no prior relationship and no next episode — it
survived being stripped of its caption and passed along by someone who did not know who made it.

**Distribution beats cadence.** Belton did not out-post anyone; Facebook put Pusheen in the compose
window. Kasing Lung did not go viral; Pop Mart put the object on shelves in dozens of countries.

**The durable money is always the same money.** Books, licensing, rights, subscriptions, physical
goods. Social was a cheap, temporary acquisition channel for some and irrelevant to others. **The
acquisition channels have all narrowed. The licensing infrastructure has not.**

### A.3 The windows that closed, with dates

Be honest about which of the above cannot be repeated. In most cases the closing can be dated
precisely.

**Instagram's static-image window — and there is almost a controlled experiment for it.** Catana
Chetwynd's own follower curve:

| Date | Instagram followers |
|---|---|
| November 2016 | 0 |
| May 2017 | ~250,000 |
| February 2019 | **2,800,000** |
| August 2026 | **3,100,000** |

**Zero to 2.8 million in 27 months — roughly 104,000 a month. Then 2.8 to 3.1 million over the
following 90 months — roughly 3,000 a month.** Growth did not slow; it stopped, while her fame
*increased* (a #1 New York Times bestseller, national tours, five books). Same creator, same
format, greater renown, a 97% collapse in growth rate. **The constraint was never the creator.**

**Tumblr's reblog graph.** Posts per quarter peaked above **100 million in early 2014** and fell to
about **30 million by October 2018** — a 70% collapse that began *four years before* the December
2018 adult-content ban usually blamed for it, with a further ~30% traffic loss after. Yahoo paid
$1.1bn in 2013, wrote down $712M by 2016, and Verizon sold to Automattic in August 2019 for a
reported ~$3M. **The mechanism that made Sarah Andersen no longer has a host platform.**

**Sticker-store slot scarcity.** Quantified above: ~100× catalogue inflation plus a ~30% economic
haircut, and Pusheen's slot was *granted by Facebook*, not earned.

**Grafolio's creator economy.** Official activity collapsed from H2 2020; the sponsorship service
was terminated in January 2021 and art products in September 2021; Naver transferred the service to
OGQ in H1 2023, relaunching as "OGQ Grafolio (Beta)" in September 2023. Every monetisation layer
that made it valuable is listed as a *past service*. **Puuung's launchpad no longer exists — which
is precisely why her 2026 campaign result matters so much.**

**What is still open**, and this is the operative list:

1. **Reddit (r/comics and equivalents)** as a cold-start ignition venue — the most reproducible
   discovery mechanic found anywhere in the study.
2. **The Chibird revenue architecture** — subscription plus physical goods plus book advances, at
   modest follower scale, reach-independent.
3. **The Stålenhag path** — a few exceptional artifacts, aggregator redistribution, a physical book
   with a real publisher, and a rights agent who sells territories and screen options.
4. **Pop Mart and the designer-toy channel** — actively signing independent artists globally.
5. **The B2B rights market generally** (Hilda) — indie publishers still fund short work by
   unknowns, and producers still read books.
6. **Andrews McMeel's open front door** — one of very few trade publishers accepting **unagented
   submissions directly from creators**, via a form with a dedicated "Books – Comics & Illustrated
   Humor" category, publishing "as many as 150 books and 200 calendars annually." GoComics is the
   scouting layer beneath it.

### A.4 Survivorship bias — the denominator, from primary sources

Everything in A.1 is a survivor. The base rate is not in that table. Here it is.

**WEBTOON Entertainment FY2025 Form 10-K, filed 5 March 2026:**

- **27 million creators** on the platform against ~157 million monthly actives.
- The filing's own glossary splits amateur from professional and states that **"the vast majority
  of the 27 million creators on our platform are amateur creators"** — meaning they have **no
  revenue-sharing agreement at all.**
- **The number of professional creators is not disclosed** — a conspicuous omission in a document
  that reports monthly paying users and ARPPU to one decimal place.
- **Global MAU declined 7.1% year over year.** Korea 27M → 24M; Rest of World 120M → 110M.
- Revenue $1.383bn (+2.5%) against a **net loss of $346.1 million** — nearly triple the prior
  year's $143.8M — including $336.5M of goodwill and intangible impairments.

Twenty-seven million creators, most structurally unmonetisable, on a platform that is shrinking and
losing a third of a billion dollars a year.

**Patreon, live figures August 2026:** **341,764 paid creators** (defined as having at least one
paying member) sharing an estimated **$25,815,605 in monthly payouts.** That is a mean of about
**$75.50 per creator per month** — before Patreon's cut, before payment processing, before tax,
before materials. Because the distribution is Pareto, **the median is far below the mean**, and the
denominator already excludes everyone who set up a Patreon and got zero members.

The correct use of this research is therefore **eliminative**: rule out mechanisms that provably do
not work at this scale, and among those that can work, pick the ones whose cost is a fixed one-off
act of drawing rather than an indefinite recurring obligation.

### A.5 The picture-book market specifically — read this before The Kits ships

The Kits is the flagship production, so this deserves its own honest section.

**The category is dominated by backlist to a degree that has no parallel.** US print sales in 2025
ran to 762.4 million units. Among the top children's titles:

| Title | Units | First published |
|---|---:|---|
| *Partypooper* (Wimpy Kid #20) | 817,430 | 2025 |
| *Dog Man: Big Jim Believes* | 767,342 | 2025 |
| *Oh, the Places You'll Go!* | 564,047 | **1990** |
| *The Very Hungry Caterpillar* | 558,815 | **1969** |
| *Brown Bear, Brown Bear* | 508,887 | **1967** |

*Goodnight Moon* (1947) sold 363,673 board-book copies. Q1 2026's number-one children's title was
***Green Eggs and Ham* (1960).** **Every top-selling picture book is backlist from 1947–1992.** A
debut does not compete against other debuts; it competes against seventy years of parental default.

**The buyer and the reader are different people, which is fatal to the follower model.** Jane
Friedman, specifically on children's books: platform is "usually low on the list of concerns," and
it is "problematic to expect children's writers to build an online following among children (their
readers)."

**And the industry has already priced this, explicitly.** A survey of 419 authors on school-visit
fees found an average full day at **$1,002**, with debut authors around $600. The premiums are
itemised: an **ALA/ALSC award adds $480/day**, a **New York Times bestseller adds $708/day**, a
booking agent adds $538/day. **There is no follower coefficient.** The children's book industry ran
this pricing exercise on itself and social media did not appear on the price list.

**The gatekeeper funnel is narrow and the numbers are public.** Fuse Literary signs roughly 10 from
about 5,000 queries (0.2%); **Greenhouse Literary, a children's specialist, signs two or three from
several thousand — under 0.1%.**

**One structural fact worth knowing before spending money on the librarian channel:** Media Source
Inc. owns *School Library Journal*, *The Horn Book*, *Library Journal* **and** the Junior Library
Guild, which selects 492 books a year. **Four apparent gates in that channel are one company.**

**The one mechanism in kidlit that demonstrably works for an independent, and what it actually is.**
Jarrett Lerner publishes hundreds of free classroom activity pages — blank comic panels, "Finish
This Comic!", how-to-draw sheets — free for educational use, and scaled the output in **March 2020**,
the exact pandemic school-closure window. Third-party uptake is verified: Travis Jonker of *100
Scope Notes*, on SLJ's blog network, writes that he has used them "for years," and Simon & Schuster
runs a featured-author page for him on its PreK-12 teachers-and-librarians portal.

**But describe it correctly: this is not audience-building. It is supply-side infrastructure for
gatekeepers.** He gives teachers free classroom labour and they reciprocate with recommendations,
purchases and visit bookings. **The audience unit is an institution, not a person.** If The Kits
ever wants a kidlit-native channel, this is the shape of it — and it is a genuinely good fit for a
studio that would rather draw than post.

## Part B — The channels, ranked

### B.1 The four tests

A generic "best platforms for artists" list optimises for reach. The binding constraints here are
different, so the tests are different. Each is a yes/no the founders can check themselves.

1. **Does the platform distribute for you** — push work to strangers without you paying or posting
   daily?
2. **Does one post keep working?** Long tail, or dead in 24 hours?
3. **Is the required unit something you would have made anyway?** A finished illustration: yes. A
   thirty-second vertical video cut to a trending sound: no.
4. **Does it survive being ignored for a season?**

**A channel that fails test 3 or test 4 is out regardless of its ceiling.**

### B.3 Notes on the top of the table

**Reddit.** The strongest evidence-backed discovery mechanic available to a studio with no
audience. It requires no graph, no history and no cadence — only one image good enough to win a
thread on its own merit. It also compounds slightly, because Reddit threads rank prominently in
Google. Use it two or three times a year at real milestones. Never as a presence.

**Instagram.** Do not expect growth; expect a function. It is the capture surface Catana used
deliberately, the place the indie illustrated-IP cohort migrated to after the comics platforms
locked out solo creators, and the account publishers and licensors check to confirm a studio is
real and stylistically coherent. An absent account is a small negative signal to exactly the people
who matter on Path 1. One post a month, roughly an hour.

**Pinterest.** Kept, but honestly labelled. The structural case is good — image search, multi-year
tail, outbound clicks by design, and a batch upload rides the seasonal clock exactly. The
evidential case is absent: nothing in this research grew through it. Four sessions a year is a
cheap bet at a fair price.

**Stickers, demoted.** The scarcity that made Pusheen and Capoo is gone and the economics are
worse. A set is still a fine Season 3 experiment — a fixed act of drawing, no cadence obligation,
and a genuine market test of whether strangers respond to these characters — but it is not a
discovery plan. **LINE Creators Market is the accessible one**, open to "everyone regardless of age
or profession," with verified format constraints: sets of **8, 16, 24, 32 or 40**, PNG only,
**≤1 MB per file**, up to 370×320px, transparent with ~10px margins; LINE Stickers Premium applies
to items on sale 180+ days. Post-2015 registrants receive **50% of net after a 30% platform fee.**

### B.4 Webtoon and Tapas — the clearest negative in the research

The obvious-looking home for illustrated IP, and the wrong one. Unusually, the evidence is decisive.

The FY2025 10-K figures in A.4 are the core of it: **27 million creators, the vast majority with no
revenue-sharing agreement at all**, a **7.1% MAU decline**, and a **$346.1M net loss**. Alongside
that, the **Creator Rewards Program was phased out in 2023** and Canvas payouts declined after; the
March 2026 "unified international CANVAS" relaunch cites **no creator counts and no earnings
figures**, which is conspicuous for a release headlined as helping creators grow earnings; and the
English market went from roughly 60 new titles a month *across all platforms* to about 40 *per
platform*.

**Tapas** is worse. Kakao Entertainment took a ~KRW 460bn goodwill impairment on it, scrapped its
IPO, closed the Korean office and sought a buyer; sister platform Radish was shut down by 31
December 2025; the creator-market side has been strategically abandoned. *(One unresolved conflict:
an April 2025 report describes Tapas Media as "in the process of liquidation" while the product
remains live. Treat it as operating-but-at-risk.)*

So: shrinking users, a dismantled rewards programme, oversupply, and a weekly-episode treadmill —
in exchange for restructuring the studio around serialising a form it does not make. Fails tests 3
and 4 together.

### B.5 Physical channels — real, but Season 3

**Design Festa (Tokyo)** — vol.64 runs **14–15 November 2026** at Tokyo Big Sight, ~6,500 booths a
day, up to 140,000 participants across two days. The structurally important feature: **no screening,
and original work only**, which makes it friendlier to original IP than any fan-art-driven
convention. The best-fitting physical event surfaced. *(Booth pricing not verified.)*

**The Seoul illustration fairs** — and the first finding is that there is no longer one of them. At
least three competing COEX events now exist plus regional spin-offs: 서울일러스트레이션페어 (SIF),
K-일러스트레이션페어, and 일러스트코리아. SIF V.21 (December 2025) drew **900+ artists and 1,000+
booths including ~50 international teams**, with weekend queues over an hour. K-Illustration Fair
publishes individual-artist booth rates of **700,000–1,200,000 KRW** (roughly US$500–870) and
explicitly lists **그림책 — picture books** as a qualifying category.

The caveat is the important part. These are **transactional fairs, not rights fairs.** Reporting
from V.21 notes the sell-outs were plush, keychains and stationery, and that **over half the
displays were animal-character and whimsical graphic work** — a perfect aesthetic fit and brutal
competition. Budget a table as merch revenue plus in-person relationship building, not as a route
to a book deal.

**Comiket** is out: 250–300k attendance per edition but overwhelmingly dōjinshi and fan-work
culture, allocated by lottery, and a poor fit for a children's picture book.

### B.6 Why the other plausible options are out

**TikTok and YouTube.** TikTok has the strongest stranger-distribution of any platform assessed and
is still out: the unit is short vertical video produced continuously, and reach collapses on
dormancy. YouTube's search-driven long tail is genuinely attractive and the most durable content of
any feed platform — but a process video is a production, not a by-product, and two artists making
monthly video would be making less art. Both fail test 3.

**Discord.** Frequently recommended, frequently a trap for two-person teams. It retains an existing
audience; it does not create one. It is a moderation obligation and a safety surface — and for a
studio whose productions may reach children, a category of risk that belongs nowhere near this
stage. It also collects far more behavioural data on an audience than an email pixel ever would,
which sits badly with the stance.

**Cara.** Values-aligned (built explicitly against AI training, NoAI metadata by default) and
therefore tempting, but small, volunteer-run, unfunded by design, with no verified user figure
since ~650–700k in June 2024. It is also the wrong room for a studio using AI-assisted art anywhere
in its pipeline.

**Tumblr.** Culturally the best fit on this list and the correct home for this art in 2014. A
mirror account costs nothing; expecting anything from it is nostalgia.

## Part C — Owned versus rented

### C.1 The cost of no owned channel is not hypothetical

**Accounts get taken and are not reliably recoverable.** The Art Newspaper documented a wave of
art-world Instagram account theft — Ukrainian artist Siggi Sekira's account was hacked in 2022 and
remained inaccessible six weeks later while the hackers messaged her followers asking for money.
Meta provides no phone, email or chat support for this. Tellingly, **the article does not mention
owned channels as mitigation at all** — every recovery path described is "appeal to Instagram and
hope."

**Whole platforms disappear, including the good ones.** Cohost — small, chronological, no algorithm,
intensely art-community, i.e. precisely the platform a studio with this ethos would choose —
announced shutdown on 9 September 2024, went read-only on 1 October, and was permanently deleted on
12 January 2025. Peak: ~227,000 registered users, 16,846 monthly actives, 3,046 paying subscribers.
About four months' notice, and no way to take contacts with them.

**Even giants go dark without notice.** TikTok suspended US service at ~22:30 EST on 18 January 2025
for roughly 13.5 hours. Survivable — but the notice period was zero.

**And whole creator economies get switched off around you.** Grafolio is the case that should
matter most here, because it is the closest analogue: a Korean creator platform that gave Puuung
her start, then terminated sponsorship (January 2021), terminated art products (September 2021), and
was transferred to another company in 2023. **Her audience survived. The platform did not.** That is
the entire argument for an owned channel, delivered by the single most relevant case in the study.

### C.2 The stance does not forbid a list — it forbids surveillance

The word "list" conflates two different things, and separating them dissolves the conflict:

- **Surveillance** — open-tracking pixels, click rewriting, device and location logging,
  behavioural segmentation, lookalike exports, retargeting. This is what the founders object to,
  and on the right platform it is *optional*.
- **An address someone chose to give you.** A person typing their email and asking to be written
  to. This is not tracking; it is the founders' own sentence, inverted.

*"Happy to be reached out to vs us trying to track everything."* A list where a reader asks to be
written to, receives four letters a year with no pixel and no link rewriting, and can leave in one
click, **is** being reached out to. Craig Mod is the existence proof at scale — fifty thousand
subscribers, a seven-figure book business, and a founder who looks at the numbers once a year.
Puuung is the existence proof at *this studio's* scale: about 1,750 people who will fund a project
a decade apart.

**And the sacrifice is smaller than it looks.** Apple holds **62.26%** of email client market share
as of July 2026, with Mail Privacy Protection affecting roughly **55–60% of all opens** — meaning
for a majority of subscribers you cannot know whether they opened anything. Switching off open
tracking in 2026 is declining to collect noise.

### C.3 The platform choice

**Buttondown. Clearly, and for a specific reason.**

- Tracking has been **off by default since 1 March 2021**, with a public, dated post from the
  founder explaining that opt-out analytics "was never an intentional design choice" and that
  "opinionated defaults matter." *(That change applied to new accounts only.)*
- Open tracking is **opt-in** — the pixel is only injected if you turn it on.
- Click tracking is **opt-in**; with it off, links are never rewritten.
- **Private mode** exists: no public subscriptions at all, subscribers added manually.
- It is the only hosted platform with a public, principled, *linkable* default that corroborates
  the studio's stance rather than merely tolerating it.

Honest caveat: Buttondown's own privacy policy is a standard SaaS policy — it collects IP, device
and usage information for internal analytics. "Collects nothing" is not literally true at the vendor
layer. What *is* true is that **no tracking artifact is placed in the subscriber's email.** Say that
precisely rather than overclaiming.

The others, briefly:

- **Substack — disqualifying.** Opens are pixel-tracked, every link is rewritten per recipient, and
  **publishers cannot turn it off.** Worse, its recommendation and Notes layer builds a
  cross-publication behavioural profile of each reader across the whole network: subscribing enrols
  readers in Substack's graph, not just the studio's list. Publishing there while claiming "we track
  nothing" would be a plain contradiction.
- **Mailchimp — the trap.** Both toggles exist, but Mailchimp's own documentation states that when a
  paid user turns off click tracking, it "will continue to redirect their URLs until certain account
  activity thresholds are met." You can stop seeing the data; you cannot reliably stop the redirect.
  A creator discovered this publicly after turning tracking off — exactly how an anti-tracking claim
  gets embarrassed.
- **Kit (ConvertKit) — poor fit.** Its documented *global* tracking settings are all about adding
  identifiers (`?ck_subscriber_id=`, auto-UTM, `sh_kit=` hashed-email link decoration), not removing
  them.
- **Ghost — good, but on by default.** All analytics can be disabled, and it is open-source and
  self-hostable, so the claim becomes auditable rather than trust-based. Its native ActivityPub work
  would let the site federate while keeping subscriber data local — genuinely interesting here, but
  still early-access with no GA date. Do not architect around it yet.
- **Listmonk — the maximal option.** A global `privacy.disable_tracking` setting now renders the
  tracking template tags inert. Correct if the studio wants the claim self-auditable and will run
  infrastructure.
- **EmailOctopus** — per-campaign toggles only, no global default. A discipline rather than a stance.

**Precedent worth citing:** Molly White's *Citation Needed* runs exactly this pattern publicly — "I
do *not* use cookies for tracking, marketing, or advertising purposes," and email open/click data
"is no longer collected by new posts or emails," with server logs kept ~14 days.

### C.4 Where the signup lives matters more than whether it exists

The `collect-nothing` note is right that a signup form on the site breaks the site's footing — a
form is a POST to a server, and that is what creates obligations.

**So do not put it on the site.** Link out to the provider's own hosted subscribe page, on the
provider's domain. The studio site keeps zero forms, zero POSTs and its four-paragraph privacy
policy; the provider carries the consent record, double opt-in and unsubscribe machinery it already
has to carry as its core business.

This is structurally identical to the decision already recorded for the AI reception in
`contact-without-collecting`: put the data-bearing surface on infrastructure built for it, keep the
studio site clean.

### C.5 RSS — ship it, and do not oversell it

RSS fits the stance perfectly: no accounts, no addresses, nothing collected, the reader subscribes
without the studio ever knowing they exist. One generated file. Ship it on day one.

It is also **the plumbing for everything else** — one feed can drive a Bluesky or Mastodon account,
a Discord webhook and an email send. One file, several channels, no recurring human act.

**But it is not the owned channel.** The one honest datum available: a technically literate blogger
who counted his own feed subscribers from server logs found **~199 RSS subscribers against 2,147
email and blog followers** — under 10%, and that is about as favourable a case as exists, since his
audience was self-selected RSS-using developers. For a picture-book audience, plan on low single
digits.

Two structural problems seal it. **RSS is not portable in the direction you need** — you cannot
export subscribers you cannot identify, so a domain change or broken feed URL loses them silently.
And **you cannot initiate**: RSS is pull-only, so for a launch on a specific date there is no way to
push.

*(A note for whoever writes site copy: a lot of circulating RSS advocacy carries fabricated numbers
— claims of 60–90% RSS "read rates" against 1–2% email open rates. The email figure alone is an
order of magnitude off every industry benchmark. Do not cite that material.)*

### C.6 Recommendation, with the honest qualification

Ship all three layers, in ascending order of effort:

1. **RSS + JSON feed — day one.** Zero cost, zero obligation, plumbing for the rest.
2. **An auto-fed Bluesky account — day one.** Marginal cost zero once the feed exists.
3. **A seasonal letter on Buttondown — four a year, from launch.** Off-site subscribe page, all
   tracking off. Four sends a year is a low enough obligation to survive contact with a working
   studio, and it *is* the grove, mailed — not a second piece of writing.

**And build the shop early, even if it is one item.** That is the Pusheen sequencing lesson and the
Chibird revenue lesson in one move: Belton was selling plush in 2011, before the traffic; Chibird's
Patreon is led by monthly physical goods, not access. A list with nothing to buy converts nothing.

**The qualification, stated because it cuts against the recommendation.** For a *traditionally
published* children's book, an author platform is largely not required — agents and publishers do
not acquire children's or fiction manuscripts on follower counts, and the school-visit fee survey
shows the industry pricing awards and bestseller status while ignoring followers entirely. The
picture-book category also has a structural ceiling on subscriber value that adult fiction does not:
families buy picture books for a limited window of years.

So the list's value here is **not primarily book marketing.** It is disaster insurance, and it is a
launch-day trigger for everything the studio sells directly — art books, merchandise, the apps, any
crowdfunding. Puuung's 1,717 backers in 2026 are exactly that asset. Start it before it is needed,
because a list started the month it is needed is worth nothing.

### C.7 The in-between options, briefly

- **An off-site "notify me" page** is a mailing list with extra steps and worse ethics — you still
  collect the address, with no ongoing relationship and no clear unsubscribe. Recommend against.
- **A Discord announcement channel** can push on a date you choose, which RSS cannot — but Discord
  holds the member graph, offers no export, can ban a server, and collects far more behavioural data
  than any email pixel. Community layer at most; never the channel of record.
- **Browser push.** Skip. Catastrophic opt-in rates, no portability, per-browser rather than
  per-person, and it reads as creepy to exactly the audience that would appreciate this stance.
- **Bluesky / Mastodon as pseudo-owned.** Better than Instagram — a custom-domain Bluesky handle is
  portable and Mastodon lets you migrate followers between instances. Neither gives contact details
  or guarantees delivery. A real hedge, not a substitute.

## Part D — Can the Forest be the outward engine?

**Short answer: the grove can be the publication. The Forest cannot be the publisher.**

### D.1 The cadence is already right

This part needs no argument. A seasonal, illustrated, dated recap of what the studio made is a
genuinely good audience-facing publication format, several small studios have used exactly it to
punch far above their size, and the studio is *already generating it* on a clock it is already
committed to. There is no second cadence to invent — exactly the outcome the brief asked for.

The precedents are real and small-scale. **BERG**, a London design studio, published numbered
"weeknotes" from Week 217 (August 2009) past Week 387 (November 2012), and that published process is
widely credited with how a small studio came to punch above its weight globally. **Panic** has
published an annual "Panic Report" for roughly two decades; one post a year, read every time. Both
are low-frequency, human-written and personality-forward. Neither is a changelog.

**That last distinction is the whole finding.** A changelog is a log. A report is a story someone
decided to tell. The Forest currently produces the first.

### D.2 Three independent reasons the auto-generated version cannot ship as-is

**Reason 1 — the audience is wrong.** Process content's natural readership is other makers:
developers, designers, illustrators. Excellent people to have — they share things and they become
collaborators — but not the readership of a children's book series. Case Study 01 found this from
the other direction: Active Theory publishes deep engineering write-ups precisely *because* it wants
technically literate readers. So the grove is a **retention and credibility instrument, not an
acquisition one.** It is the reason to come back, not the front door.

**Reason 2 — unedited AI prose under the studio's name is a measurable cost in 2026.** Roughly **7%
of consumers say visible AI-generated content makes them trust a brand more, against 31% who say it
makes them trust it less**, and 91% expect disclosure. In publishing specifically, a 2025 YouGov
survey found majorities of US readers disapproved of AI use across all five scenarios tested, and
**61% said they would feel less fulfilled** on learning a book they had read was AI-written. For a
studio whose entire pitch is *"an entity that makes things beautifully and thoughtfully,"* shipping
unedited machine prose as its public voice contradicts the pitch in the most visible possible place.

**Reason 3 — Google's spam policy describes this shape by name.** Google defines scaled content
abuse as "when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and
not helping users," and its first listed example is "using generative AI tools or other similar
tools to generate many pages without adding value for users." A generated recap page per month per
production, indefinitely, is *formally* that shape. It is rescued by being genuinely useful and
human-edited — not by intent, which Google cannot see. Four substantial human-edited groves a year
is a very different footprint from forty-eight machine-written saplings.

### D.3 The editorial layer, and exactly what it costs

The pipeline's genuinely valuable work — selecting and sequencing real, already-committed,
human-made art — needs no human help and should not get any. That part is already both the
interesting part and, per the synthesis, the legally strongest part. What needs a human is the
prose, and the judgement about what the season was actually about.

**The grove pass — roughly two hours, four times a year:**

1. **Decide the season's one true story** (~10 min). The human picks the through-line; the pipeline
   cannot know which of forty commits was the moment something changed.
2. **Rewrite the opening and closing paragraphs in the founders' voice** (~30 min). Everything
   between can stay machine-assembled; readers judge a piece by its first and last paragraph.
3. **Cut it by half** (~20 min). Auto-generated recaps over-report by nature — they treat every
   commit as equally worth mentioning. Deletion is most of the editing.
4. **Choose one hero image** (~10 min), ≥1200px and 16:9, for the social card and Discover
   eligibility.

Roughly **eight hours a year**, and it converts the grove from an artifact into a publication. It
also becomes the seasonal letter, so the same two hours serves both.

There is a second, unadvertised benefit. The synthesis already notes that AI-written recap prose is
thin on copyright protection, and that "a human editing pass converts it into human-authored
expression." Publishing outward is exactly the moment that starts to matter commercially. **The same
two hours buys audience quality, trust, and protectability.**

### D.4 Keep the saplings private

Monthly saplings stay where they are — behind the Field, quiet, unedited, a working record and the
engine's development environment. Only the seasonal grove goes outward, edited, four times a year.
That split also keeps the published surface small enough that the scaled-content concern in D.2
never arises.

## Part E — Discovery for the site itself

### E.1 The realistic baseline, said first

A brand-new domain with no backlinks, in a category with no search demand — nobody types "midori
fuwafuwa" into Google before hearing the name somewhere else — will get essentially no organic
search traffic for a long time. Everything below is about not being *invisible* and not *squandering*
the arrivals other channels produce. Hygiene, not a growth channel. Budget a few days once, then
nothing.

### E.2 The one setting that could silently break everything — check it before 15 September 2026

**This is the most urgent item in this document and it has a date on it.**

Cloudflare replaced its single "Block AI bots" toggle on **1 July 2026** with three behaviour
categories, each set independently to *Block on all pages* / *Block on pages with ads* / *Allow*:

| Category | Cloudflare's definition |
|---|---|
| **Search** | "any behavior that collects or indexes your content, so it can answer questions about it later" |
| **Agent** | "automated behavior that is acting, usually in real time, on a person's behalf" |
| **Training** | "a crawler taking your content to train or fine-tune models… your data is permanently absorbed" |

Path: **Security Settings → Configure AI bot policies** (also via AI Crawl Control → Security).
Available on all plans including Free.

**The footgun:** Cloudflare states that multi-purpose crawlers "such as Googlebot, Applebot, and
BingBot will be blocked by customers who have selected to block Training," because the most
restrictive applicable rule wins. **Setting Training to "Block (on all pages)" blocks Googlebot.**
For a studio whose entire problem is that nobody knows its name, this is the single setting capable
of silently de-indexing the site. Never set it.

**From 15 September 2026**, new domains onboarding to Cloudflare get Training and Agent blocked by
default *only on pages that display ads*, with Search allowed; the legacy toggle retires the same
day. An ad-free art site means those defaults are effectively a no-op — the danger is over-tightening
them by hand.

**The right lever is managed robots.txt** — Security Settings → Bot traffic filter → "Set your
preference to block training in robots.txt." It injects `Disallow: /` for exactly the *training*
bots (`GPTBot`, `ClaudeBot`, `Google-Extended`, `CCBot`, `Bytespider`, `Amazonbot`,
`Applebot-Extended`, `meta-externalagent`) plus a `Content-Signal: search=yes, ai-train=no,
use=reference` line — and deliberately omits `OAI-SearchBot`, `Claude-SearchBot` and
`PerplexityBot`. **That is already exactly the "AI search yes, AI training no" posture the studio
wants.** If a robots.txt already exists, Cloudflare prepends rather than overwrites.

Confirmed from the vendors' own documentation:

| Bot | Purpose | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| `OAI-SearchBot` | surfaces sites in ChatGPT search | **allow** |
| `GPTBot` | OpenAI training | block |
| `Claude-SearchBot` | indexes for Claude web search | **allow** |
| `ClaudeBot` | Anthropic training | block |
| `PerplexityBot` | search results, explicitly not training | **allow** |
| `Google-Extended` | robots.txt token only; Gemini/Vertex training and grounding | block |

OpenAI is explicit that "sites that are opted out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT
search answers," and that blocking GPTBot does not remove you from ChatGPT search — the two are
independent. Google states `Google-Extended` "does not impact a site's inclusion in Google Search
nor is it used as a ranking signal."

**One honest consequence to accept:** Google's documentation makes no mention of AI Overviews, which
are served from the regular Search index via Googlebot. **There is no way to opt out of AI Overviews
without leaving Google Search entirely.**

Also decide **`ai-input`** explicitly in the Content Signal — Cloudflare deliberately leaves it
blank, and it governs whether the studio's descriptions of its own characters can ground AI answers.
For a studio nobody has heard of yet, being *absent* from the systems people increasingly ask
questions through is a real cost.

### E.3 The JS problem is solved by construction — do not un-solve it

The plan already specifies Astro static generation. That decision, more than any SEO technique, is
the answer to "how does a JS-heavy immersive site avoid being invisible": the meaningful content is
in the delivered HTML before any script runs, and the parallax is a progressive enhancement.

The failure mode to guard against is *regression*. Two rules:

- **Every word that matters must exist in view-source.** Test by disabling JavaScript entirely. If
  the reduced-motion baseline — already designed first per Case Study 01 — is a complete, readable
  page, the crawler problem is solved permanently.
- **Never render meta tags client-side.** Social crawlers do not run JavaScript at all; a
  client-rendered `og:image` is simply absent.

A subtler distinction worth building around: **content that exists in the HTML but is `opacity: 0`
or transform-offscreen until a scroll handler fires is *present* to a parser, whereas content
injected by JS on scroll is *absent*.** Those look identical to a human and completely different to
a crawler. Prefer the former everywhere.

None of the AI-crawler vendor documentation — OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity — states whether their
crawlers execute JavaScript. That silence is itself the finding: **assume none of them do.**

### E.4 Social preview cards are the highest-value item here

For this studio the Open Graph card *is* the discovery surface. When someone shares a Field in a
message, a Discord server or a Bluesky post, the card is what strangers see — and for an
illustration studio the card can carry actual art, which is a far better advertisement than any text
snippet.

- **A distinct, real illustration per page** as `og:image` — 1200×630, an absolute URL with full
  scheme and host. Not the logo. A Field's card should be that Field's art.
- `og:title`, `og:description`, `og:type`, `og:url`, `og:image:alt`, and `og:image:width` /
  `og:image:height` — Facebook's docs recommend the dimensions specifically "to ensure that the image
  loads properly the first time it's shared."
- `twitter:card` = `summary_large_image`. *(Whether X still requires it in 2026 could not be
  verified — its card docs 404'd. Ship it; it costs nothing and other consumers read it.)*
- **Bluesky is the special case, and it is unusual.** Its embed record is built by the *posting
  client* and frozen into the post at post time; Bluesky never re-scrapes the page. Two hard
  consequences: **fixing `og:` tags later does not fix already-posted cards** (the only remedy is
  delete-and-repost), and there is no cache to bust because there is no cache. The lexicon caps the
  thumbnail at **1 MB** — for a studio pushing high-resolution art, the constraint most likely to
  bite. Export social thumbnails well under 1 MB.
- **Pinterest Rich Pins survive**, reduced to three types — Article, Product, Recipe. Article is the
  only plausible fit. The only tag Pinterest documents explicitly is the opt-out
  (`<meta name="pinterest-rich-pin" content="false" />`); markup is read from Open Graph or
  schema.org.
- Facebook's Sharing Debugger at `developers.facebook.com/tools/debug/` "also triggers a scrape of
  your page" — the re-scrape mechanism when a card is wrong.

**Ship the cards before announcing anything.** Every platform except Bluesky caches aggressively, and
Bluesky does not cache at all — so on every platform, for different reasons, a wrong card at launch
is expensive to fix.

### E.5 Google Discover — free eligibility, do not count on it

Worth two lines of work because Google's documentation is explicit that there is no gatekeeping:
"content is automatically eligible to appear in Discover if it is indexed by Google and meets
Discover's content policies," and "no special tags or structured data are required." Image
requirements: at least 1200px wide, more than 300,000 total pixels, 16:9, and `max-image-preview:large`
enabled.

Set `max-image-preview:large` sitewide and make hero images meet the spec — work the design system
requires anyway. Then forget about it. Discover is a lottery weighted heavily toward news and
lifestyle publishers.

### E.6 Structured data — a short list

- **`Organization`** on the hub, with `name`, `logo`, `url` and `sameAs` pointing at every official
  profile. This is what consolidates the studio's identity across the web. Highest value of the set.
- **`Person`** for Seonso and Grumpy Carrot, linked from the Organization — they are the entities
  people will search by name.
- **`Book` / `BookSeries`** on The Kits' Field once there is an ISBN. Premature before that.
- **`BreadcrumbList`** if the Field/Forest hierarchy warrants it.

⚠️ **Do not plan around the sitelinks searchbox** (`WebSite` + `SearchAction`) — widely reported
deprecated. `WebSite` itself is still worth shipping for name/url; the searchbox feature is not.

### E.7 llms.txt — ship half of it

The spec moved to **version 2 on 10 August 2026**. Honest verdict: **no primary evidence exists that
any AI provider's crawler retrieves or respects a third-party llms.txt.** Every confirmed publisher
of one is publishing it for their own developer documentation.

For an art studio the *index file* is near-worthless. But **v2's markdown link-relation half is
cheap and addresses a real problem**: `rel="alternate" type="text/markdown"` (via HTML `<link>` or
HTTP header) offers a plain-text rendering to crawlers that cannot parse a parallax-heavy DOM. Ship
the link relation; skip the index file.

### E.8 The item nobody would put in an SEO section, and the best one here

**Submit the site to the web design awards.** Awwwards charges **$65 per submission**; FWA is the
other one that matters. Codrops runs no submission form but does publish Studio, Designer and
Developer Spotlights and can be pitched directly by email.

A one-off act rather than a channel, but unusually well-aimed for *this* studio. The site is being
built as an award-calibre immersive piece with a genuinely novel premise — the studio's own Dreamland
as literal information architecture, per Case Study 01. The audience at those sites is designers and
developers, the most link-prone population on the internet. A feature produces two things a new
domain cannot otherwise get: a concentrated burst of high-quality visitors, and **permanent
high-authority backlinks**, which is the actual answer to the no-backlinks problem in E.1.

The odds are not trivial — roughly 15,000 submissions a year against fewer than 365 Sites of the Day
— but $65 against that upside, for a site whose entire design premise is "this should be worth
looking at," is the best-value line item in this document.

**And it is the same mechanic that worked for Stålenhag.** Boing Boing featured him on 21 August 2013
and aggregators redistributed his paintings. The modern equivalent of an art-blog feature, for a
studio whose most shareable artifact is its own website, is a design-award feature. Same shape,
verified precedent.

### E.9 Analytics — what "did it work" looks like without tracking

Cloudflare Web Analytics is already settled, and it answers the only question that matters: **the
referrer report.** Once a season, look at where arrivals came from.

Two honesty notes. Referrer data has degraded across the web — a large share of arrivals report as
direct, particularly from mobile apps and messaging, so Pinterest and messaging-app traffic will be
systematically undercounted. And there is no way to know how many people saw a pin and did nothing.
Accept both.

**Check quarterly, not daily.** Daily analytics for a studio on a seasonal clock produces anxiety and
no decisions. Craig Mod checks once a year.

## Part F — What to do first

The design rule: **the outward work must be a by-product of the inward work, never a second job.**
Nothing here asks for a daily habit.

### Before launch — one-time technical work, then never again

1. **Open Cloudflare's AI bot policies and look at the actual values** (E.2). Do this before 15
   September 2026. Never set Training to *Block on all pages*. Turn on managed robots.txt. Decide
   `ai-input` deliberately.
2. Static-render everything. Verify by loading the site with JavaScript disabled and confirming the
   reduced-motion baseline is a complete, readable page.
3. Per-page Open Graph and Twitter card tags, server-rendered, with a real 1200×630 illustration per
   Field. **Every social thumbnail under 1 MB** (Bluesky's hard cap).
4. `max-image-preview:large` sitewide; hero images ≥1200px, 16:9.
5. `Organization` + `Person` structured data on the hub.
6. RSS and JSON feeds, sitemap, image sitemap, plus the `rel="alternate" type="text/markdown"` link
   relation.
7. Cloudflare Web Analytics on.
8. **Claim the handles everywhere**, including platforms you will never use. Free, takes an hour, and
   prevents someone else being "Midori Fuwafuwa" on a platform later. Trademark hygiene — it belongs
   alongside `trademark-launch-checklist`.
9. Choose the channels and **delete the rest from the plan on purpose**, in writing, so the decision
   does not get re-litigated every season.

### At launch — one week of work, once

1. **Submit to Awwwards ($65) and FWA.** Pitch Codrops by email.
2. Open the Buttondown account — off-site subscribe page, all tracking off. Announce it once.
3. **Put one thing in the shop.** Even a single print. The Pusheen lesson is that the shop must exist
   before the traffic, not after.
4. Post the site once on each chosen channel — as an image, not a link dump.
5. Set the Bluesky account to auto-post from RSS.
6. **Do not launch The Kits in the same season as the site.** One launch per season.

### Season 1 — build the habit, ignore the numbers

- One **artifact of the month**, three times: a single finished, self-contained, shareable image.
  That is the entire recurring content obligation. It is one drawing.
- One **grove** at season's end, through the two-hour editorial pass (D.3), mailed as the letter.
- One **Instagram post per month** — the same artifact. No Reels.
- One **Pinterest batch upload** of the season's art.
- Open Cloudflare Analytics **once**, at season's end, and look only at referrers.

### Season 2 — the habit plus one deliberate shot

- Same rhythm, unchanged.
- Plus **one Reddit post** — the single best artifact of the year, posted to r/comics or the closest
  appropriate community, at a real milestone. This is the one evidence-backed cold-start mechanic
  still open, and Catana's case shows what it can do from zero. One shot; do not become a presence.
- Begin the Path-1 legibility work, which is a few emails rather than a project: **Andrews McMeel
  accepts unagented submissions directly from creators**, and **Pop Mart actively signs independent
  artists worldwide.** Both are open front doors that cost a submission, not a campaign.

### Season 3 — the first outward artifact that is not an image

By now there is a year of groves, a coherent body of character work, a shop, a list, and real
evidence about which characters people respond to. That is precisely the package Path 1 needs to
look at.

Pick **one**, not all:

- **A LINE Creators Market sticker set** — a fixed act of drawing, no cadence obligation, a genuine
  market test. Demoted from a discovery bet to an experiment, for the reasons in B.3.
- **Design Festa** (Tokyo, mid-November) — no screening, original work only.
- **A Seoul illustration fair** — perfect aesthetic fit, brutal competition, ~US$500–870 a booth,
  transactional rather than a rights fair.
- **A Kickstarter.** Two numbers worth knowing: overall, 277,302 funded projects and $8.71bn pledged
  as of April 2025; and Wharton's Ethan Mollick found projects **featured on the Kickstarter homepage
  have an 89% chance of success against 30% without** *(correlational — featured projects are chosen
  partly for momentum and polish)*. Puuung's 2026 result — $100,238 from 1,717 backers — is the
  realistic shape of a good outcome at this studio's scale.
- **A rights agent.** Stålenhag's screen deals came from Salomonsson Agency, not from followers.

There will be another season. Pick one.

### The standing rules

- Never more than one launch per season.
- Never more than one channel experiment at a time.
- Never look at analytics more than once a season.
- If a channel produces nothing in three seasons, drop it and write down that you did.

## Conclusion

The gap the brief identified is real, and the honest answer is smaller and stranger than expected.

**Every social acquisition window in this research has closed, and most of them can be dated.**
Facebook's sticker slots were *granted*, not earned, and there were seven of them. Tumblr's reblog
graph lost 70% of its posting volume between 2014 and 2018. Catana Chetwynd went from 104,000
followers a month to 3,000 a month while becoming more famous. LINE's sticker catalogue inflated a
hundredfold. Grafolio switched off its creator economy entirely. **The mechanisms still open in 2026
are the ones that never depended on an audience at all** — Pop Mart signing independent artists,
Andrews McMeel's unagented front door, a rights agent selling territories, Nobrow commissioning an
unknown into a 24-page format, Scholastic's book fairs.

So the plan is not a following. It is a body of work good enough that a distributor eventually wants
it, and — meanwhile — the small thing the studio actually controls. Chibird supports a full-time
career on 574,000 followers because the money is subscriptions, physical goods and book advances,
and she went full-time in 2021, after every window had already shut. Puuung raised roughly the same
amount from roughly the same 1,750 people in 2026 as in 2015, having lost her original platform
entirely in between. Craig Mod turned fifty thousand people who asked to hear from him into a
seven-figure book business while checking the numbers once a year.

**That is the shape to build: a letter four times a year, a shop that exists before it is needed, one
drawing a month on Instagram, one deliberate Reddit post a year, and a Pinterest upload each season.**
Behind them, the grove — edited by a human for two hours a season, which is the reason people come
back rather than the reason they arrive, and which quietly converts machine output into something the
studio actually owns.

The stance survives all of it intact. "Happy to be reached out to" and "a person asked to be written
to" are the same sentence. And with Apple mangling 55–60% of email opens anyway, switching off the
trackers costs almost nothing real — it declines noise and keeps the principle.

The honest part belongs in the plan rather than a footnote. **This compounds slowly and there is no
version that does not.** Kasing Lung spent fifteen years illustrating picture books, three years on a
toy line that underperformed, four more before Pop Mart, and five more before the queues. The studio's
advantage is not a channel. It is that it has already committed to a rhythm designed to be sustained
for exactly that long.

## Open items — not verified in this pass

Flagged rather than guessed, in the house convention. *(The session's research budget was exhausted
mid-pass, and several Korean and Japanese domains were unreachable.)*

1. **LINE Creators Market revenue split and payout terms** for a new registrant. The 50%-of-net-after-
   30%-fee structure is verified from LINE's own help page; **payout thresholds, eligible countries
   and tax withholding are not published anywhere reachable.** KakaoTalk Emoticon Studio and Naver OGQ
   Market could not be reached at all. **The load-bearing unknown is residency** — business
   registration (사업자등록), a Korean bank account or identity verification may gate Kakao, OGQ and
   Tumblbug simultaneously. Gates the Season 3 sticker option.
2. **Pinterest's current scale and outbound-click behaviour**, and whether Rich Pins still require
   validation or domain claiming. The structural argument holds regardless; it is a cheap bet either
   way.
3. **Cloudflare's 15 September 2026 scope.** Cloudflare's own text supports "new domains onboarding";
   a secondary source claimed broader scope including existing free customers. **Check the dashboard
   rather than trusting either source.**
4. **Whether `WebSite` + `SearchAction` sitelinks searchbox is formally deprecated** (E.6).
5. **Whether Andrews McMeel is still signing genuinely new social-media creators.** Its open
   submissions form and 2026 releases are verified, but both new titles are from existing creators.
6. **Tumblbug fees and non-Korean eligibility.** On structure alone the most promising Korean
   crowdfunding platform for a picture-book-plus-character-goods studio, and the least researched.
7. **Award eligibility by territory.** The Caldecott is restricted to artists who are US citizens or
   residents with the book published by an American publisher in the US — **if the studio is not
   US-based this is simply unavailable**, which removes one argument for chasing traditional US
   publication. Confirm the equivalent rules wherever the studio actually operates. This matters
   because the school-visit survey shows awards are the thing the industry actually prices.
8. **Bookshop distribution economics, if self-publishing.** The real gate is not the ISBN: on
   IngramSpark, being stockable realistically means a **53–55% wholesale discount plus returnable
   status**, and returns cost the publisher the full wholesale price plus $3/book US or $20
   international shipping. KDP prints a 32-page premium-colour picture book at a **$3.60 fixed cost**
   with no per-page charge, forcing a minimum list around $6.00 at the 60% royalty rate. Verified; the
   surrounding items — ISBN pricing, consignment terms, library review channels — were not.
9. **Artist alley economics.** No creator-published sell-through numbers were obtainable. Do not
   budget a fair table without them.
10. **Liz Climo** could not be verified this pass and is excluded from the case set. Note that she was
    a professional Simpsons animator before the comics — an industry-backed case, not an independent
    audience-building one, if it is ever added.
